The strategy, as coded

Your rules were translated into a mechanical system so they could be tested honestly. Each stock gets an independent $100,000 sleeve.

  • Starter position. 20% of the sleeve buys shares on day one — you "own the company" from the start.
  • Buy the ladder. When price falls below its trailing 2-year high, tiers trigger: −20% deploys 15% of capital, −30% deploys 20%, −40% deploys 25%, −50% deploys 40%. Deeper tiers get bigger budgets and cheaper prices, so share counts grow fast as it falls — your averaging-down rule. Tiers re-arm after price recovers to within 10% of its high.
  • Sell the top lot. Any day the price exceeds the cost of the highest-cost open lot by 20%+, that lot is sold — cascading down to the next lot if it also qualifies. Cheap lots are kept; expensive lots are recycled.
  • Options variant — puts. Each month, sell a cash-secured put struck at the next unfilled buy tier. Assigned = you bought your tier, plus premium. Expires worthless = keep the premium, tier stays armed.
  • Options variant — calls. Each month, sell a covered call on the highest-cost lot struck at 1.2× its cost. Called away = your 20% sell executed, plus premium.

Assumptions: split-adjusted daily closes with dividends credited as cash; idle cash earns 2.5%/yr; option premiums estimated with Black–Scholes at 1.1× trailing 63-day realized volatility, monthly expiries, no early assignment; no taxes, commissions or slippage. The "good fundamentals, not overvalued" screen is not mechanically coded — buying only at −20% to −50% off the highs is its price-side proxy. See caveats.

What drove the results

1 · The sell rule is the expensive half

Re-running the backtest with the buy ladder intact but the 20% sell rule removed (buy dips, never sell) transforms the outcome:

Same buy discipline, same entries — but letting winners run instead of recycling them after +20% takes NFLX from $295k to $1.24M and NVDA from $366k to $18M. The dip-buying half of your strategy is sound; the mechanical +20% rotation is what forfeits compounding. ENPH is the honest counterexample: there, selling was the right call (it beat never-selling), because the stock ultimately gave back 95%.

2 · Cash drag is the quiet cost

Waiting for −20% corrections in names that rarely correct means the average sleeve was only invested. Cash earning 2.5% can't keep up with equities compounding at 13–27%. A bigger starter position (50% instead of 20%) helps modestly; combining it with never-selling gets the basket to $6.5M — most of the way to buy-and-hold, while still buying every crash.

3 · The options overlay genuinely helps

Across the five sleeves the overlay collected in premium ( from puts, from calls) and improved every sleeve except NFLX, where a called-away lot missed part of a rebound. It works because it's paid for doing what the strategy would do anyway: the put's assignment is your tier buy; the call's exercise is your 20% sell. On the highest-volatility name it mattered most — ENPH's premiums alone were $53k, lifting that sleeve from 295% to 465%.

4 · Averaging down cannot rescue a broken business

ENPH is also the warning label. The ladder deployed its full $100k by mid-2015, then rode the stock down 95%; the strategy's own drawdown was −95%, identical to buy-and-hold. A fixed-capital ladder runs out of ammunition after the −50% tier — below that you are simply holding. This is where your "solid companies, good fundamentals" screen has to do the real work, and it's the part a price-only backtest cannot capture: averaging down is only as safe as the business underneath it.

Round 2 — making the strategy better

Round 1 exposed two leaks: idle cash earning 2.5% while waiting for corrections, and the +20% rotation selling compounders just as they get going. Round 2 tests five levers that keep the strategy's discipline — buy corrections in size, recycle expensive lots, sell puts and calls — while plugging the leaks:

  • Trend filter on the sell rule. The +20% lot rotation only fires while price is below its 200-day average — harvest bounces inside downtrends, but let confirmed uptrends run.
  • Park idle cash in SPY. The waiting capital holds the index instead of cash; tier buys are funded by selling SPY. You're never out of the market.
  • A never-sold core. 30–50% bought on day one and exempt from rotation — "own the company" permanently; the ladder trades only around it.
  • Raise the trigger to +50%. Same rotation, more room before a lot is called done.
  • Size tiers off current sleeve value instead of the original $100k, so buys stay meaningful as the sleeve compounds.

What the ladder shows

The trend filter is the single best free change. Changing nothing but when the +20% rule may fire takes the basket from $291k to $951k — and it beat SPY on both return (21.4% vs 13.1% CAGR) and drawdown (−27% vs −31%). NVDA's sleeve alone went from 11.9% to 28.6% CAGR while its drawdown improved to −23%, because the 2022 bottom lots were held through 2023 instead of being sold on the first +20% bounce.

SPY parking cures the cash drag. On its own it more than doubles the outcome ($663k) while average single-stock exposure stays around 24% — the defensive character survives; the waiting capital just stops being dead weight. Its cost: your "dry powder" now falls in the same crashes you're buying into (basket drawdown −33%).

The options overlay is asymmetric in the improved versions. Cash-secured puts kept earning their keep — in the trend + parked variant they lifted NVDA from $3.0M to $6.4M by forcing bigger buys at the 2018 and 2022 lows. Covered calls, though, work against the trend filter: they cap exactly the recoveries the filter is trying to ride (the growth combo scored higher without them). If you run the improved rules, keep the puts; write calls only on lots you'd genuinely be happy to lose at that strike.

The never-sold core is the bridge to buy-and-hold. A 50% permanent core takes the basket to $5.0M at 40% CAGR — but drawdowns climb back to −46%, and by then you're mostly a buy-and-hold investor with a trading sleeve. Round 3 — the growth combo, out of sample

The round-1 stocks were picked knowing they (mostly) won. This round takes the best-performing rule set and runs it on a deliberately harder list — an oil major, a fallen GLP-1 star, two struggling-then-recovering chip names, a cyclical packager, a micro-cap boom-bust stock, and a Chinese giant that went nowhere for a decade: XOM, NVO, INTC, QCOM, AMKR, AEHR, BABA. If the combo only worked on hindsight winners, this is where it should fall apart.

The exact rule set tested

  • 30% core, never sold. Bought on day one, exempt from every sell rule — the "own the company" anchor.
  • Tiers sized off current sleeve value. −20/−30/−40/−50% below the 2-year high deploy 15/20/25/40% — of what the sleeve is worth now, so buys stay meaningful as it compounds.
  • Trend-filtered rotation. Non-core lots sell at +20% over cost only while price is below its 200-day average; uptrends run untouched.
  • Waiting capital lives in SPY, sold down to fund tier buys and put assignments.
  • Cash-secured puts, no covered calls. Monthly puts struck at the next unfilled tier (round 2 showed calls fight the trend filter, so they're dropped). Assignment = the tier buy, plus premium.

What the deep dive shows

The combo's habitat is exactly this list. Every winner here is a stock that crashed hard at least once and eventually recovered: the ladder bought XOM through the 2020 oil collapse (13.3% CAGR vs 7.1% holding), bought INTC's 2022–24 implosion in size before the 2025–26 recovery ($567k vs $317k), compounded AMKR's repeated semiconductor cycles ($1.46M vs $858k), and turned BABA — 2.0% a year for a decade holding — into 8.0% by buying every leg down and harvesting every dead-cat bounce below the 200-day line. QCOM shows the quieter version: 20 tier buys, zero sells (never 20% above cost below trend), $62k of dividends swept into SPY — a grind that still beat holding by half.

The puts earned $131k across the basket and their keep on the cyclicals. AMKR was the showcase: 13 assignments and $40k of premium turned the combo's $1.04M into $1.46M — paid repeatedly to catch a knife that kept bouncing. INTC's 8 assignments added $157k. But the overlay is genuinely two-sided: on NVO the puts subtracted $35k (assigned into a decline that kept declining), and a cross-check on the round-1 stocks shows puts roughly neutral there ($4.35M vs $4.79M without) — on a relentless compounder, waiting a month for assignment costs more than the premium pays. Puts monetize chop; they don't improve rockets.

NVO is the loss that teaches the most. The one stock where buy-and-hold won ($271k vs $230k): the 30% core rode the −70% collapse in full, the tiers and puts kept averaging into a business whose earnings story was actually breaking, and the trend filter never got a bounce big enough to harvest. It's ENPH's lesson from round 1 wearing a different jersey — this rule set has no defense against a structural decline, only against volatility.

And note what the combo no longer is: defensive. Basket max drawdown was −60% — identical to buy-and-hold. Running ~80–95% exposure with a permanent core means the growth combo wins by buying lows harder, not by dodging crashes. The drawdown protection lives back up the round-2 ladder (the original rules held the basket to −19%); you can have protection or compounding, and the ladder prices each step.

Bottom line of the deep dive: on a fair list containing losers and flatliners, the growth combo added value on 6 of 7 names and +71% at the basket level — its edge comes from disciplined oversized buying in crashes and paid patience (puts) on cyclical names, not from picking winners. Its two known failure modes: structural decliners (NVO, ENPH) where averaging down compounds the damage, and its drawdowns are buy-and-hold-sized. The fundamentals screen — is the business intact? — remains the one rule this backtest cannot automate.

Caveats

  • Hindsight ticker selection. These five were chosen knowing how they turned out. NVDA's 448× is not a repeatable expectation; equally, ENPH's −95% shows what the same list looks like when it goes wrong.
  • No fundamental screen. Historic P/E-vs-peers valuation gating wasn't backtested (clean point-in-time fundamentals aren't freely available); the deep-correction requirement is only a partial proxy.
  • Option prices are modeled, not quoted. Black–Scholes on realized vol understates crash premiums and ignores early assignment, spreads and strike granularity. Treat overlay results as directionally right, not precise.
  • No taxes or costs. The sell rule and option assignments generate constant short-term realized gains — in a taxable account the gap to buy-and-hold widens further.
  • Fixed-dollar tiers. Budgets are percentages of the original $100k, not of the growing sleeve, which understates later-year compounding of the strategy.

Takeaways

Read as a study of the mechanics, not as advice:

  • This is a risk-management strategy, not a return-maximizing one. It cut the basket's worst drawdown from −53% to −19% and never came close to blowing up — at the cost of trailing even a plain index fund.
  • Its natural habitat is boom-bust, mean-reverting names — volatile cyclicals like ENPH or CVX — not steady compounders. Applied to MSFT or NVDA, the 20% rotation systematically sells the future.
  • A hybrid resolves the tension — and Round 2 above backtests it: a trend filter on the sell rule plus index-parked cash lifted the basket from 9.6% to 27.9% CAGR, and a never-sold core carries it to ~40% at buy-and-hold-like drawdowns. Each step up the ladder buys return with drawdown; the table makes the price explicit.
  • The options overlay earns its keep whenever you would genuinely buy at the put strike and genuinely sell at the call strike — it monetizes patience you already have. It's most valuable exactly where the strategy itself works best: high-volatility names.
  • The fundamentals gate is the load-bearing wall. The backtest shows the price rules can't distinguish a dip from a decline — that judgment has to come from the business, which is the part of your strategy no ladder can automate.
